# Concept Note: Causal Evaluation of AI-Assisted Care Decisions

## Question

Do recommendations generated by a health-system AI model change care decisions and improve patient outcomes?

Health systems increasingly use predictive models to identify patients for outreach, review, and care-management services. Existing evaluations generally measure discrimination, calibration, and predictive fairness. Those metrics do not establish whether a model changes human decisions or whether the resulting care improves outcomes.

## Proposed setting

UC Davis Health's population-health model predicts future emergency-department visits and unplanned hospitalizations. Patients at or above an operational risk-percentile threshold enter a human review process that may lead to outreach and care-management enrollment. The threshold is motivated partly by staff capacity.

This workflow creates a sequence that can be evaluated empirically:

\[
\text{risk score}
\rightarrow
\text{eligibility flag}
\rightarrow
\text{human review}
\rightarrow
\text{outreach and enrollment}
\rightarrow
\text{health and utilization outcomes}.
\]

## Empirical design

The initial design compares patients immediately above and below the operational threshold. Under continuity around the cutoff, the discontinuity in outcomes identifies the local causal effect of algorithmic eligibility. Discontinuities in review, outreach, and enrollment reveal how the recommendation changes human and institutional behavior.

The primary estimand is the total effect of algorithmic eligibility. A secondary fuzzy-RD analysis may estimate the effect of care-management enrollment if eligibility affects outcomes only through that recorded intervention and does not reduce anyone's probability of treatment.

## Policy value

The local effect at the cutoff has an operational interpretation. After accounting for outreach and treatment costs, it measures the marginal value of expanding AI-based eligibility. This can inform whether the health system should change the threshold, increase staffing, redesign the human-review process, or target patients by expected treatment benefit rather than untreated risk.

## Distributional analysis

The evaluation will prespecify a small number of population groups and distinguish:

- Predictive calibration.
- Probability of review and enrollment conditional on eligibility.
- Causal effects on utilization and health outcomes.
- Net benefits after resource costs.

Multiplicity adjustments will accompany analyses across groups and outcomes.

## Information needed for feasibility

The design requires patient-level linkage of:

- Raw score, percentile, model version, and scoring timestamp.
- Operational cutoff and recommendation exposure.
- Review, override, outreach, contact, enrollment, and service intensity.
- Emergency-department visits, hospitalizations, mortality, and costs.
- Staffing, caseload, and alternative routes into care management.

The first step does not require patient-level data. Aggregate counts and action rates in narrow score bins around the threshold can establish whether the cutoff creates a usable first stage.

## Expected outputs

1. An empirical evaluation of the effect of AI-generated eligibility on decisions and outcomes.
2. Econometric methods for inference at learned algorithmic decision boundaries.
3. A reproducible monitoring framework for future model versions and population groups.
