# Formal Model, Version 0.2

## 1. Scope

The baseline model studies a static decision system in which a fixed AI model generates a continuous score, an institution converts the score into an eligibility recommendation, and a human decision-maker chooses a downstream action. The baseline deliberately avoids a fully structural model of human information processing. Potential actions are sufficient to define the policy effects and establish identification.

The first methodological paper should answer one question:

> What does a discontinuity at an AI-generated decision boundary identify about the effect and marginal policy value of the deployed decision system?

The methods paper now prioritizes training-induced boundary uncertainty. Dynamic model updating, interference, multiple actions, and sequential monitoring remain extensions.

## 2. Environment

Index individuals by \(i\). Let:

- \(X_i\) denote observed characteristics available to the AI model.
- \(G_i\) denote a prespecified population group relevant for distributional analysis.
- \(S_i\) denote the AI-generated score.
- \(c\) denote the operational decision cutoff.
- \(Z_i\) denote algorithmic eligibility or a positive recommendation.
- \(D_i\) denote the human or institutional action.
- \(Y_i\) denote a valued downstream outcome, normalized so that higher values are better.
- \(C_i\) denote the resource cost generated by the policy.

The score is

\[
S_i=g(X_i;\widehat{\eta}),
\]

where \(g\) is trained before the evaluation period. The baseline conditions on the realized scoring rule and assumes it remains frozen during evaluation.

The institution applies the threshold policy

\[
Z_i(c)=\mathbf{1}\{S_i\geq c\}.
\]

The human decision-maker chooses an action

\[
D_i(z)\in\{0,1\}.
\]

The setup allows incomplete compliance:

\[
P(D_i(1)=1)<1
\quad\text{and}\quad
P(D_i(0)=1)>0.
\]

In a care-management application, \(Z_i\) may indicate eligibility for review, while \(D_i\) indicates successful enrollment or receipt of services.

## 3. Potential outcomes and primary estimands

Let

\[
Y_i(d,z)
\]

denote the outcome if the downstream action is set to \(d\) and algorithmic eligibility is set to \(z\). Allowing both arguments prevents the model from imposing an exclusion restriction prematurely. An AI flag may trigger chart review, clinician notification, patient contact, or documentation in addition to the recorded treatment.

Define the total potential outcome under eligibility state \(z\) as

\[
Y_i^z=Y_i(D_i(z),z).
\]

The primary causal estimand is the local effect of algorithmic eligibility:

\[
\tau_Y(c)
=
E[Y_i^1-Y_i^0\mid S_i=c].
\]

This is the intention-to-treat effect of the complete decision system for individuals at the operational margin.

The behavioral first stage is

\[
\pi_D(c)
=
E[D_i(1)-D_i(0)\mid S_i=c].
\]

The first stage measures whether the AI-generated recommendation changes human or institutional behavior.

## 4. Baseline assumptions

### Assumption A1: Consistency

Observed outcomes and actions equal the potential values corresponding to realized eligibility:

\[
D_i=D_i(Z_i),
\qquad
Y_i=Y_i^{Z_i}.
\]

### Assumption A2: Continuity

For \(z\in\{0,1\}\), the conditional means

\[
E[Y_i^z\mid S_i=s]
\quad\text{and}\quad
E[D_i(z)\mid S_i=s]
\]

are continuous in \(s\) at \(c\).

The economic content is that individuals immediately above and below the cutoff would have evolved similarly in the absence of the change in algorithmic eligibility.

### Assumption A3: Positive and continuous score density

The score has density \(f_S(s)\) that is positive and continuous near \(c\).

Precise manipulation of inputs, administrative recoding, or changes in who receives a score could invalidate this condition or the continuity assumption.

### Assumption A4: Frozen decision rule

The scoring function, feature construction, cutoff, and recommendation logic remain fixed over the local evaluation window.

This assumption is stronger than necessary, but it separates the baseline result from the generated-score and adaptive-model extensions.

### Assumption A5: Local support

There are sufficient observations on both sides of \(c\), and the downstream outcomes are observed using the same definitions and follow-up windows.

## 5. Identification results

### Proposition 1: Effect of algorithmic eligibility

Under Assumptions A1–A5,

\[
\tau_Y(c)
=
\lim_{s\downarrow c}E[Y_i\mid S_i=s]
-
\lim_{s\uparrow c}E[Y_i\mid S_i=s].
\]

The right-minus-left discontinuity identifies the local causal effect of being made eligible by the AI decision rule.

#### Proof sketch

Immediately to the right of \(c\), realized eligibility equals one; immediately to the left, it equals zero. Consistency maps observed outcomes to \(Y_i^1\) and \(Y_i^0\). Continuity maps the two limits to conditional potential-outcome means at the same score value.

### Proposition 2: Behavioral first stage

Under the same assumptions,

\[
\pi_D(c)
=
\lim_{s\downarrow c}E[D_i\mid S_i=s]
-
\lim_{s\uparrow c}E[D_i\mid S_i=s].
\]

This result separates an ineffective recommendation from an ineffective downstream intervention. A zero outcome effect has different interpretations when the recommendation strongly changes behavior and when it is largely ignored.

### Additional assumptions for a treatment LATE

To interpret the fuzzy-RD ratio as the effect of a single treatment, add:

#### Assumption A6: Exclusion

\[
Y_i(d,z)=Y_i(d).
\]

Eligibility affects the outcome only through the recorded action \(D_i\).

#### Assumption A7: Monotonicity

\[
D_i(1)\geq D_i(0)
\quad\text{almost surely near }c.
\]

A positive recommendation does not make any individual less likely to receive treatment.

### Proposition 3: Treatment effect for recommendation compliers

Under Assumptions A1–A7 and \(\pi_D(c)>0\),

\[
\tau_{\mathrm{LATE}}(c)
=
\frac{\tau_Y(c)}{\pi_D(c)}
=
E[Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)\mid D_i(1)>D_i(0),S_i=c].
\]

The project will treat this ratio as secondary. In operational systems, eligibility frequently triggers several actions, making exclusion doubtful.

## 6. Marginal policy value

Let individual net value under eligibility state \(z\) be

\[
V_i^z
=
\omega(G_i)Y_i^z-\lambda C_i^z,
\]

where \(\omega(G_i)>0\) is an optional distributional weight and \(\lambda>0\) converts resource costs into outcome units.

Institutional welfare under cutoff \(c\) is

\[
W(c)=E[V_i^{Z_i(c)}].
\]

The eligible share is

\[
q(c)=P(S_i\geq c)=1-F_S(c).
\]

Define the local net effect

\[
\Delta_V(c)=E[V_i^1-V_i^0\mid S_i=c].
\]

### Proposition 4: Marginal value of expanding eligibility

If the conditional net-value functions and score density are continuous at \(c\), then

\[
\frac{dW}{dq}\bigg|_{q=q(c)}=\Delta_V(c).
\]

Thus, the discontinuity at the cutoff identifies the marginal welfare effect of expanding algorithmic eligibility, provided expansion is accompanied by the resources needed to preserve treatment intensity.

#### Proof sketch

Write welfare as

\[
W(c)
=
\int_{s<c}E[V_i^0\mid S_i=s]f_S(s)\,ds
+
\int_{s\geq c}E[V_i^1\mid S_i=s]f_S(s)\,ds.
\]

Differentiation gives

\[
\frac{dW}{dc}
=
-\Delta_V(c)f_S(c).
\]

Because \(dq/dc=-f_S(c)\), the chain rule yields the result.

### Policy interpretation

- \(\Delta_V(c)>0\): expanding eligibility at the margin increases welfare.
- \(\Delta_V(c)=0\): the current cutoff satisfies a local first-order condition.
- \(\Delta_V(c)<0\): marginal eligibility is harmful or too costly.

The proposition does not establish global optimality. Net benefits need not be monotone in the score.

## 7. Risk prediction versus treatment-benefit targeting

For this section, let \(A_i(d)\) be an adverse outcome and define

\[
r(x)=E[A_i(0)\mid X_i=x]
\]

as untreated risk. Let

\[
b(x)=E[A_i(0)-A_i(1)\mid X_i=x]
\]

be expected treatment benefit, and let \(k(x)\) be expected treatment cost. Net benefit is

\[
n(x)=b(x)-k(x).
\]

Suppose capacity permits treatment of share \(q\) of the population.

### Proposition 5: Optimal targeting criterion

Among deterministic policies that treat population share \(q\), welfare is maximized by treating individuals with the highest values of \(n(X_i)\).

A policy that ranks individuals by predicted untreated risk \(r(X_i)\) is optimal only when its selected set coincides with the set selected by net treatment benefit. A sufficient condition is that \(n(x)\) is weakly increasing in \(r(x)\).

#### Proof sketch

If a policy treats person \(a\) and leaves person \(b\) untreated while \(n(X_b)>n(X_a)\), swapping their treatment assignments increases expected welfare. Repeating these pairwise improvements yields selection by net benefit.

### Implication

Better risk prediction need not produce better policy. Calibration, discrimination, treatment responsiveness, human implementation, and capacity constraints are distinct objects.

## 8. Human discretion extension

A later extension can model the action as

\[
D_i(z)
=
\mathbf{1}\{m(H_i,S_i,z,G_i)-K_i\geq 0\},
\]

where \(H_i\) is private information observed by the human decision-maker, \(m(\cdot)\) is perceived benefit, and \(K_i\) is a perceived or institutional action cost.

This formulation can study whether discretion:

- Uses information absent from the AI score.
- Weakens or strengthens the recommendation first stage.
- Improves targeting by treatment benefit.
- Produces group differences conditional on the same score and recommendation.
- Changes with experience, explanations, or alert fatigue.

The baseline paper does not require identification of \(m(\cdot)\).

## 9. Priority methodological extensions

### 9.1 Generated running variable

If \(g\) is estimated on an independent training sample and frozen, inference can condition on the realized score function. This identifies the effect at the boundary created by the deployed model. It does not automatically cover uncertainty about which boundary a different training sample would create.

The primary methods extension separates evaluation-sample uncertainty from training-induced variation in the local causal effect. Candidate approaches include sample splitting, cross-fitting, nested resampling, joint influence-function expansions, and uniform inference over a class of learned boundaries. See `methodological-contribution.md` for the formal research program.

### 9.2 Model updates and version changes

Let

\[
S_{it}=g_t(X_{it};\widehat{\eta}_t),
\qquad
Z_{it}=\mathbf{1}\{S_{it}\geq c_t\}.
\]

The research problem is to construct a comparable running variable and valid local estimand when both the model and cutoff change.

### 9.3 Capacity constraints

If staff capacity is \(K\), welfare is \(W(c,K)\). Lowering \(c\) while holding \(K\) fixed may dilute treatment intensity for existing participants. Proposition 4 describes an eligibility expansion that preserves the treatment technology; empirical work must measure staffing and caseloads to defend that interpretation.

### 9.4 Group-specific thresholds and causal equity

Predictive calibration by group does not imply equal causal benefit. Define

\[
\tau_{Y,g}(c_g)
=
E[Y_i^1-Y_i^0\mid S_i=c_g,G_i=g].
\]

The project will distinguish predictive fairness, equality of access, equality of treatment effects, and distributionally weighted welfare. Multiple-testing adjustments are required when examining many groups and outcomes.

### 9.5 Sequential post-deployment monitoring

Repeated evaluation across time, sites, model versions, and outcomes creates dependent multiple testing. This extension connects the project to hierarchical testing and confidence sequences.

## 10. Threats to interpretation

1. **No discontinuous first stage:** the operational threshold may not change review or treatment sufficiently.
2. **Administrative sorting:** cases may be recoded or routed differently around the threshold.
3. **Outcome contamination:** prediction targets may be mechanically reused as outcomes.
4. **Other eligibility routes:** patients below the AI threshold may enter the program through clinical referral.
5. **Resource interference:** treating more patients may reduce service intensity for others.
6. **Score-version mixing:** identical numerical scores may have different meanings across model versions.
7. **Missing recommendation logs:** stored scores alone do not prove the recommendation was shown.
8. **Selective outcome observation:** actions induced by the model can change testing, diagnosis, and recorded outcomes.

## 11. Simulation plan

The first simulation should vary four features independently:

1. Predictive quality of \(S_i\) for untreated adverse outcomes.
2. Correlation between untreated risk and treatment benefit.
3. Human compliance with algorithmic eligibility.
4. Staff capacity and treatment dilution.

Core outputs should show:

- Predictive AUC and calibration.
- Recommendation first stage.
- Outcome discontinuity.
- Fuzzy-RD treatment effect when exclusion holds.
- Marginal net benefit of expanding eligibility.
- Group-specific effects with multiplicity-adjusted uncertainty.

The simulation should demonstrate concretely that predictive improvement and policy improvement can move in opposite directions.

## 12. Version 0.2 decision

The central methods paper will distinguish:

1. The conditional effect at the boundary of the model actually deployed.
2. The algorithm-averaged effect across plausible training samples or model versions.
3. The welfare value of the deployed risk ranking relative to net-benefit targeting.

This structure makes the benchmark RD transparent while locating the new contribution in the randomness and instability of learned policy boundaries. The first simulation implements the conditional benchmark and the risk-versus-benefit targeting result. A second simulation will add repeated model training and compare conditional with two-source confidence intervals.
